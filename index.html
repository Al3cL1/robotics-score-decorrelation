Learning to be Different

Component Specialization for Compositional Diffusion Policies

Alec Laprevotte, Haonan Chen, Xiaoshen Han, Yilun Du
Harvard University
arXiv (coming soon) Code

Summary

Diffusion policies are increasingly built by composing \(K\) components and summing their score predictions into a single composed score to create modular and adaptable systems. In practice, however, the components are not supervised individually during training, so they end up redundant. As such, we introduce a drop-in score-decorrelation loss, added to the diffusion objective with weight \(\lambda\), that penalizes overlap between the components' noise predictions \(\epsilon_{\theta_i}\):

\[ \mathcal{L}_{\text{decorr}} = \mathbb{E}\Big[\tfrac{2}{K(K-1)} \sum_{i<j} \big(q_i^\top q_j\big)^2\Big], \qquad q_i = \frac{|\epsilon_{\theta_i}|}{\|\epsilon_{\theta_i}\|_2 + \delta} \]

In the 2D navigation task shown here, increasing \(\lambda\) forces the two components to align with the coordinate axes (up/down - left/right). As cosine similarity falls and the factors become interpretable, they can then be recombined to solve out-of-distribution 1D and 3D navigation tasks.

Component Specialization

Without decorrelation, all four factors contribute almost equally to every action dimension. With decorrelation, they divide the action space: factor 2 drives the mobile base, factor 3 controls the gripper, and factors 1 and 4 share the end-effector motion.

Composite Tasks

When a factorized policy is trained on multi-stage tasks, adding decorrelation lets it complete more of each composite's sub-goals, even in rollouts that fail to finish the full sequence. This indicates the gains come from reusable factorized structure rather than memorized composite trajectories.
